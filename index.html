Where do I find the request?

  • In your wallet's confirmation screen, look for a Data, Hex, or View raw tab and copy what it shows.
  • For a message to sign, copy the message text itself.
  • Developers: copy the request object (method + params) from your dapp or the browser console.

Act now — minutes matter. Drainers often use a stolen permission within seconds, but not always. If anything is still in your wallet, start with step 1 immediately.

  1. Is your whole wallet compromised?

    Your wallet is fully compromised if you did any of these:

    • Typed or pasted your seed phrase or private key into a website, form, or "support" chat
    • Signed a raw hash (eth_sign)
    • Approved an EIP-7702 "upgrade" or delegation that a website asked for

    If so, skip straight to step 3. Revoking will not help, because the attacker controls the wallet itself.

  2. Cut off the attacker's access

    If you only signed an approval, permit, or listing, revoke it. Revoking costs a small gas fee. Use a revoke tool such as revoke.cash or your wallet's own approvals page.

    • Token approval: revoke the approval for the spender you don't recognise.
    • Permit2 signature: revoke the token's approval to the Uniswap Permit2 contract (0x0000…8BA3). That cancels every Permit2 permission for that token, including the one you signed.
    • Plain permit (gasless approval): it can't be cancelled directly. Move that token to a new wallet (step 3). Once the balance is gone, the permit is useless.
    • NFT "approve all": turn off the collection approval for the unknown operator.
    • NFT listing (OpenSea, Blur): cancel your listings on that marketplace. On OpenSea, use "Cancel all listings".

  3. Move what's left to a brand-new wallet

    Create a new wallet with a new seed phrase. A new account inside the same wallet shares the same seed, so it is not safe. Move the most valuable assets first.

    If gas money you send to the old wallet disappears immediately, a "sweeper bot" holds your key. Stop sending funds to it. Only specialist rescue methods (for example, Flashbots bundles) can get assets out, so get help from a well-known security community, never from a stranger who contacts you.

  4. Watch out for recovery scams

    Anyone who messages you offering to "recover your funds" is almost certainly a scammer. Real helpers never ask for your seed phrase, a "fee" up front, or remote access to your computer.

  5. Record and report

    Save the website address, screenshots, and transaction hashes. Report the scam address at chainabuse.com, tell the community or project that was impersonated, and file a police report. In the US, also report at ic3.gov.

Why it matters

Signatures are where wallets get drained

No gas, no warning

Signing a message is free and nothing shows up on-chain, so it feels harmless. But a signed permit or order can be used later to take your tokens or NFTs.

Permissions outlive the moment

Approvals and permits often have no limit and no expiry. A site you used once can still move your funds months later.

Names can be faked

The app name inside a request is written by the website. Plainsign checks the actual contract addresses instead.
